# Why Have Housing Prices Gone Up?

*Edward L. Glaeser, Joseph Gyourko, Raven Saks — American Economic Review, 2005 (paraphrased summary for testing).*

## The puzzle

Across the United States, the price of a house and the cost of building that
house have pulled apart. In the most expensive metros, homes sell for far more
than the cost of the structure plus a modest land value. The standard story
blames a physical shortage of land — but land is not scarce in most of the
country, and even in coastal metros the implied price of an extra quarter-acre
is larger than raw land markets can explain.

## The regulatory account

The paper's argument is that the wedge between price and construction cost is
mostly the shadow price of **regulation**: zoning, minimum lot sizes, permitting
delay, and community review. Each restriction lowers the elasticity of housing
supply — the responsiveness of new building to price — so that when demand
rises, quantity cannot adjust and price absorbs the shock instead.

## Why elasticity is the hinge

Two metros can face the same demand growth and diverge sharply in price if one
can build and the other cannot. Where supply is elastic, prices stay near
construction cost. Where it is inelastic, prices climb without bound relative to
cost. The policy implication is that affordability is, to first order, a supply
problem created by rules, not a land-scarcity problem created by geography.

## Caveats

The account is about the long run and the metro scale. It does not deny that at
the parcel scale, or in the short run, new building can coincide with rising
local rents; nor does it claim regulation is never worth its cost.
