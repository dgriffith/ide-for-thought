# The induced-demand objection

New market-rate housing, on this view, does not lower rents where it is built —
it *raises* them. A shiny new building signals that a neighborhood is arriving,
draws in higher-income renters and the amenities they spend on, and the local
rent gradient tilts up. The supply was real, but so was the demand it summoned.

This [[alternative-to::notes/upzoning-lowers-rents]] shares the same facts —
more units were permitted, rents still climbed — but reads the causation the
other way: the units are a *marker* of demand, not a brake on price.

Two things it gets right. First, at the **parcel** scale the local effect can
genuinely be positive: a luxury tower can lift its immediate block even as it
relaxes the metro-wide market. Second, filtering is slow; the renter priced out
this year is not consoled by cheaper units a decade hence.

Where it overreaches is the leap from "local, short-run, parcel-level" to
"metro-wide, long-run." That leap is the crux with
[[notes/upzoning-lowers-rents]].
